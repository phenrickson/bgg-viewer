The Predictions room — a natural way to sort and surface upcoming games

A sortable table of upcoming games. The model's five outputs are all here as columns — but rendered as one thing: plain right-aligned numbers. The sort column is heavier and full-contrast; every other column sits back in muted. No bars, no meters, no badges.

Real data: the top 22 of 4,347 games published 2026, by predicted geek rating. Nothing is curated.

The layout question this is meant to settle: rail or filter bar.

What it costs: ~14rem off the table. With five numeric columns holding fixed widths, that comes entirely out of the game's metadata line — publisher truncates first.

What the filter bar buys

Full width for the table, which is where the metadata line gets to say designer and publisher and categories. For an unreleased game those are everything you know — 99.7% have a publisher, 83% a designer.

What it costs: a second structural idiom, and the facet lists collapse into dropdowns that hide their counts. Card Game 612 in Layout A's rail is itself a fact about the year.

Why every number renders the same

Five model outputs, five different encodings — a bar, a meter, a second bar, two bare counts — read as five unrelated claims and made the row a dashboard. They are the same kind of thing and now look like it.

The only differentiation left is the sort column: heavier, larger, full contrast, while the rest sit in muted. Re-sort and the emphasis moves with it, so the column you are reading by is always the one that reads first. That is a typographic distinction, and it costs no ink.
