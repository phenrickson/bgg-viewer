What the filter bar buys

Full width for the table, which is where the metadata line gets to say designer and publisher and categories. For an unreleased game those are everything you know — 99.7% have a publisher, 83% a designer.

What it costs: a second structural idiom, and the facet lists collapse into dropdowns that hide their counts. Card Game 612 in Layout A's rail is itself a fact about the year.

Why one mark and not five

The first pass gave three columns three different marks in three different hues — a continuous bar for predicted geek, a five-segment meter for complexity, a second bar for hurdle. All three encode magnitude, which is a sequential job, and a sequential job takes one hue. Three hues for three magnitudes is a rainbow, and the hue was carrying "which column" — something the header and the column position already say. Three geometries for one job on top of that, and the row reads as a dashboard rather than a table.

So: one geometry (a 3px track, square at the baseline, 2px rounded at the data end), one hue (--chart-1, the app's existing single-series colour), and it appears on exactly one column. Because a mark needs a stated domain to mean anything, the header carries it — 4.5–7.0, 0–100%, 1–5 — and each is honest to the column it sits under.

Putting it on the sort column is what makes it earn its ink: within a page of 22 rows the numbers are close together, and the bar is what turns "6.93, 6.92, 6.90…" into a shape you can read down.

What this resolves

The RatingBar domain problem. Its 5.5–8.8 is hardcoded and empties on a population whose median is 5.46. A mark whose domain is declared per column, in the header, doesn't inherit that — and the domain is visible rather than assumed.

P. Ratings never gets a mark under any sort. It runs 25 → 38,495 with no natural maximum, and a bar without a bounded domain is decoration. Sorting by it emphasises the column typographically and leaves the track off.
